Recorded lectures and educational videos are now a normal part of studying, whether your course posts recordings or you're learning from YouTube on your own. But watching a lecture again is a slow way to revise, and "I'll rewatch it before the exam" rarely happens. Transcripts give you a second way in: text you can skim, search, annotate and test yourself on. This guide sets out a complete study workflow built around them.

## Why transcripts help students

- **Speed.** Most people read considerably faster than lecturers speak, so reviewing a transcript takes a fraction of the lecture's runtime.
- **Searchability.** Find the exact point where a concept was defined instead of guessing which lecture it was in.
- **A second channel.** Reading what you've just heard catches words and ideas you missed, especially with fast speakers, unfamiliar accents, or poor audio.
- **Accessibility.** For students who are deaf or hard of hearing, or who process text more easily than speech, transcripts are essential rather than optional.

The risk is using them passively — reading a transcript feels productive but, on its own, doesn't build lasting memory any better than rewatching. The workflow below is designed around *active* use.

## The four-pass workflow

### Pass 1: Preview (5 minutes, before watching)

Extract the transcript on the [Transcriptly home page](/) and switch to **Paragraphs** with **long** paragraphs. Read only the first sentence of each paragraph. You're building a rough map of the lecture: what topics come up, in what order.

Write three questions you expect the lecture to answer. They don't have to be good questions — their job is to give you something to listen for.

### Pass 2: Watch actively

Watch the lecture with the video, not the transcript, as your focus. Keep notes minimal: timestamps of moments that confused you or seemed important, and answers to your preview questions. Don't try to write everything down — you have the transcript for that.

### Pass 3: Review in text (same day or next day)

Now open the transcript properly.

1. Go to each timestamp you flagged. The **Timestamped** view shows each segment with its start time, so you can find the passage and reread the explanation at your own pace.
2. Use the in-transcript search to find every place a key term appears. Lecturers often define a term once and then use it differently later.
3. Fix any obvious caption errors in your notes — especially terminology. Auto-captions are often weakest on exactly the technical words you need. See [How to Fix Incorrect YouTube Captions](/guides/how-to-fix-incorrect-youtube-captions).
4. Write structured notes from the transcript. [How to Turn YouTube Lectures into Structured Study Notes](/guides/how-to-turn-youtube-lectures-into-study-notes) walks through this step in detail.

### Pass 4: Test yourself (spread out over the following weeks)

Turn the lecture into questions, then answer them from memory before checking the transcript. For example:

- *Definition:* "What does the lecturer mean by 'opportunity cost'?"
- *Explanation:* "Why does the lecturer say price controls cause shortages?"
- *Application:* "How would that argument apply to rent control?"

Answering from memory — not rereading — is what makes material stick. Space the sessions out (for example, after a day, a few days, then a week or two). [How to Prepare Exam Notes from Educational Videos](/guides/how-to-prepare-exam-notes-from-educational-videos) covers building a full question bank.

## Saving lectures as books

If you're following a whole course, save each transcript to your library with **Save to My Library** (a free account is needed). Rename them with a consistent prefix — `BIO 201 L04 — Cell signalling` — so your dashboard search finds the whole course with one word. In the reader you can:

- Read page-by-page or in continuous scroll, in light, sepia or dark themes.
- Pick up where you stopped, since reading progress is saved.
- Tap an unfamiliar English word to see its meaning and save it — useful if you're studying in a second language.
- Export a PDF to print and annotate by hand.

## Studying in a second language

Many students take courses in a language that isn't their first. Transcripts help a lot here:

- Read the transcript **before** watching so the vocabulary is familiar when you hear it.
- Use **Side-by-Side** translation mode to check your understanding of difficult passages — but study from the original, since machine translation can distort technical meaning.
- Build a word list from the lecture vocabulary. See [How to Build a Personal Vocabulary Dictionary from Video Transcripts](/guides/how-to-build-a-vocabulary-dictionary-from-video-transcripts).

## Group study

Transcripts make group work more efficient:

- Split a long lecture by timestamp ranges, with each person writing notes for one section.
- Use the transcript as the shared reference when you disagree about what was said.
- Turn the combined notes into a shared question bank.

Share your notes and questions rather than full transcripts of copyrighted lectures, unless your course allows it.

## Common mistakes

- **Replacing the lecture with the transcript.** Diagrams, demonstrations and emphasis are often only in the video. Use both.
- **Highlighting instead of testing.** Highlighting a transcript feels like work but is one of the least effective study methods on its own.
- **Trusting auto-captions on technical terms.** Check them against your textbook or the slides.
- **Leaving review until the exam.** One review the next day is worth more than three the night before.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I use transcripts for lectures recorded on platforms other than YouTube?

Transcriptly extracts from YouTube links. If you have a subtitle file (`.srt` or `.vtt`) or plain text from another platform, you can upload or paste it on the home page under **Upload / Paste Subtitles** to read it as a book.

### Is it allowed to transcribe my university's lectures?

Check your institution's policy. Many allow personal study use of recordings but restrict sharing. Never redistribute a lecture transcript without permission.

### How long does the review pass take?

As a rough rule, plan for about a third to half of the lecture's length for a careful review in text, depending on how dense the material is.
