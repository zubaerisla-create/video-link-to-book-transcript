A growing share of primary material lives in video: conference talks, expert interviews, public hearings, lectures, documentaries. Video is slow to search. You can't skim it, you can't text-search it, and quoting from it means scrubbing back and forth to catch the exact words. Transcripts fix most of that. This guide covers how to use them to screen sources quickly, find what you need, quote accurately and cite in a way others can check.

## 1. Screen before you watch

The biggest time saving comes before you watch anything. Instead of watching the first ten minutes of twenty candidate videos, extract their transcripts and skim.

1. Extract each candidate on the [Transcriptly home page](/) (or with the [Chrome extension](/guides/how-to-use-chrome-extension) for many videos in a row).
2. Use **Paragraphs** view with **long** paragraphs and read only the first sentence of each paragraph. That's usually enough to see the structure of the talk in a couple of minutes.
3. Use the in-transcript keyword search for your key terms. If a 45-minute talk never mentions the concept you're researching, you've saved 45 minutes.
4. Sort the candidates into *relevant*, *maybe* and *not relevant*. Only watch the relevant ones, and only the relevant sections.

## 2. Search smarter than a single keyword

Speech uses different words from writing. Searching a transcript for the formal term often misses the passage where a speaker explained it informally. For each concept, list:

- The **formal term** ("confirmation bias").
- **Informal phrasings** ("people only look for evidence that agrees with them").
- **Likely mishearings** in auto-captions, especially for names and jargon. "Kahneman" might appear as "Conman" or "Carnaman". Search for the first few letters, or for a nearby word that's easy to recognise.
- **Related examples** the speaker might use instead of naming the concept.

## 3. Extract evidence with context

When you find a relevant passage, capture it in a consistent format so it's usable later. A simple evidence card:

```
Source:    [Speaker], "[Video title]", [Channel], [Upload date]
URL:       https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XXXXXXXXXXX&t=754s
Timestamp: 12:34–13:10
Captions:  auto-generated (checked against audio: yes)
Quote:     "..."
Context:   Answering a question about...; earlier at 08:15 he qualifies this by...
My note:   Supports / contradicts / complicates [claim]
```

Two fields matter more than they seem:

- **Captions** — record whether the text came from auto-generated or creator-uploaded captions, and whether you checked the quote against the audio.
- **Context** — speakers often qualify or reverse a claim a few minutes later. A quote without context can misrepresent them.

## 4. Verify every quote against the audio

This is the step people skip, and it's the one that matters most. Auto-captions frequently:

- Drop or invert negations ("can" vs "can't" is a classic).
- Mishear numbers ("fifteen" vs "fifty").
- Mangle names, places and technical terms.
- Merge or split sentences in ways that change emphasis.

Before you quote anything, open the video at the timestamp and listen. YouTube URLs accept a start time: add `&t=754s` (or `?t=754` on `youtu.be` links) to open at 12:34. The **Timestamped** view or SRT export gives you that time — see [Understanding Transcript Languages, Tracks and Timestamps](/guides/understanding-transcript-languages-and-timestamps) for how the times are measured.

If you're working from a translated transcript, never quote the translation as the speaker's words. Quote the original and give your own (or a professional) translation, labelled as such.

## 5. Cite timestamps properly

Citation styles differ in the details, but they all want enough information to find the source and the moment. A typical pattern:

> Speaker Surname, First name. "Title of Video." *Channel Name*, YouTube, Day Month Year of upload, URL. Timestamp.

Practical notes:

- Use the **upload date**, not the date you watched.
- Give the **timestamp range** for quotes in your notes, even if your style only needs the start.
- If the speaker isn't the channel owner, name the speaker.
- Use a link that includes the start time if your format allows it.

Check the current guidance of the style you're using (APA, MLA, Chicago and others all publish rules for online video).

## 6. Keep a research log

For any project longer than a week, keep a running log — a simple dated text file is enough:

```
2026-10-28  Screened 14 talks on X. 5 relevant (see index). Key new term: "Y".
2026-10-29  Verified 6 quotes against audio. One misquote in captions at
            talk-07 12:34 ("can" → "can't"). Corrected in notes.
```

It makes write-up easier and shows how you reached your conclusions.

## A realistic example

Imagine you're writing about how product teams talk about user research. You find twelve conference talks.

1. **Screening (30 minutes):** You extract all twelve and keyword-search for "interview", "user research" and "talk to users". Seven mention the topic substantively.
2. **Targeted watching (90 minutes):** You read the relevant sections of the seven transcripts, then watch only those sections — perhaps a fifth of the total runtime.
3. **Evidence (45 minutes):** You create fourteen evidence cards. Checking against the audio, you correct two quotes where captions dropped a "not".
4. **Organisation:** Cards go into your notes tool, tagged by theme, with timestamps that let you return to any of them in seconds.

The numbers will vary by project, but the shape is consistent: transcripts move time from *watching to find* to *reading to understand*.

## Common mistakes

- **Trusting auto-captions for quotes.** Always verify.
- **Losing the timestamp.** Paragraph text has no times; keep the segment view or an SRT/JSON export.
- **Ignoring speaker identity.** Captions rarely label speakers. In panels and interviews, confirm who said it.
- **Quoting a translation as original speech.**
- **Over-relying on search.** Keyword search finds words, not ideas. Skim the structure too.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it acceptable to cite a YouTube video in academic work?

Usually yes, if the source is credible for your purpose — a recorded lecture by a subject expert is very different from an anonymous commentary channel. Evaluate the speaker as you would any author.

### Can I cite the transcript instead of the video?

Cite the video; the transcript is your tool for working with it. If you corrected the captions, your quote should match what was said in the audio.

### What if the video gets deleted?

Note it in your log, keep your evidence cards, and if possible find an archived copy or an alternative source. This is one reason to record the upload date and channel.
