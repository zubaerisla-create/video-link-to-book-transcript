Some lectures are worth more than notes. A brilliant two-hour talk, a lecture series that explains a subject better than any textbook, an interview you'll want to come back to — sometimes you want the whole thing, but in a form you can read comfortably. This guide covers how to turn long video lectures into reading material: what changes between speech and text, how much editing is worth doing, and how to read the result on any device.

## Speech is not prose

The first surprise when you read a transcript is how different speech looks on the page. Even a polished lecturer:

- **Repeats themselves** for emphasis, because listeners can't reread.
- **Uses fillers** — "so", "right?", "you know", "um".
- **Starts sentences and abandons them** halfway.
- **Signposts heavily** — "now, the second thing I want to say is…".
- **Refers to things you can't see** — "as you can see on this slide", "this one here".

None of this is a flaw; it's how spoken explanation works. But it means a raw transcript reads more slowly than a book. The question is how much to change it.

## Three levels of reading material

| Level | Effort | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Clean transcript** | Minutes | Readable paragraphs, every word preserved |
| **2. Light edit** | 20–40 minutes per hour of lecture | Fillers and false starts removed, headings added |
| **3. Full adaptation** | Several hours | Reads like a written chapter; structure reorganised |

For most purposes, level 1 or 2 is the sweet spot. Level 3 is essentially writing a new text and only makes sense for material you'll reread many times or share (with permission).

## Level 1: A clean transcript in minutes

1. Paste the lecture link into the [Transcriptly home page](/) and extract the transcript.
2. Switch to **Paragraphs** and choose **long** paragraph length (around 180 words per paragraph). This produces the most book-like flow.
3. Click **Save to My Library** (a free account is needed) to open it as a book.

Transcriptly handles the mechanical problems of captions: it removes the repeated words caused by rolling auto-captions, joins fragments into continuous text, and starts new paragraphs at sentence boundaries after natural pauses or once a paragraph is long enough. It deliberately does *not* rewrite the speaker's words. [How Sentence Splitting Works in Multilingual Transcription](/guides/how-sentence-splitting-works-in-multilingual-transcription) explains the process in detail.

For many lectures, this is all you need.

## Level 2: A light edit

If you'll reread the material several times, a light edit pays off. Export the transcript as **TXT**, open it in any editor, and work through it in one pass:

1. **Add headings** at each topic change. Lecturers' signposts ("Let's move on to…") tell you where.
2. **Delete fillers and false starts.** "So, um, what I, what I want to say is that memory is reconstructive" → "Memory is reconstructive."
3. **Collapse repetition.** Keep the clearest version of an idea explained three times.
4. **Replace visual references.** "As you can see here" → "[Slide: graph of forgetting over time]" or a short description. Add the timestamp so you can look at the slide.
5. **Fix caption errors**, especially names and technical terms. See [How to Fix Incorrect YouTube Captions](/guides/how-to-fix-incorrect-youtube-captions).

Don't change the meaning or tidy away the speaker's hedges ("probably", "in most cases"). If you want to add your own comments, mark them clearly, for example in square brackets.

Then bring the edited text back into your library: on the home page, choose **Upload / Paste Subtitles**, upload the `.txt` file (or paste the text), and give it a title.

### Before and after (illustrative)

**Clean transcript (level 1):**

> So, um, the thing about forgetting — and this is, this is really important — the thing about forgetting is that it's not a failure, right? It's actually, in a sense, it's actually the system working. Because if you remembered everything, you'd have no way to tell what mattered. So as you can see on this graph, it drops off really steeply at first…

**Light edit (level 2):**

> **Forgetting as a feature**
>
> Forgetting is not a failure — in a sense, it's the system working. If you remembered everything, you'd have no way to tell what mattered. [Slide at 23:40: forgetting curve, steep early decline.] Memory drops off very steeply at first…

The words that remain are the speaker's; the edit just removes what only made sense aloud.

## Reading comfortably

Transcriptly's reader is designed for long texts:

- **Page-by-page** reading gives natural stopping points; **continuous scroll** is better for skimming.
- **Light, sepia and dark** themes suit different lighting — sepia is easier on the eyes for long sessions for many readers.
- **Adjustable text size** makes phone reading practical.
- **Reading progress** is saved, so a two-hour lecture can be read over several sittings.
- **Word lookup** lets you tap unfamiliar English words for a definition and a Bangla translation.

For print, or to read on an e-reader, export a **PDF** from the saved book. It includes a title page and page numbers, and renders Bangla script correctly.

## How to read a lecture well

- **Read with a question.** Before you start, write down what you want to understand. It focuses your attention.
- **Pause at section ends.** Summarise each section in a sentence before moving on.
- **Jump to the video for visuals.** When the text refers to a diagram, open the video at that timestamp.
- **Reread selectively.** The second read should be the important sections, not the whole lecture.

## When reading isn't the right format

Some videos lose too much as text: demonstrations, lab techniques, art and design critique, maths worked on a whiteboard without narration. For these, use the transcript as an index into the video rather than a replacement.

## Common mistakes

- **Over-editing.** Smoothing every sentence can erase nuance and introduce your own interpretation.
- **Deleting visual references without a note.** You'll lose track of where the diagram was.
- **Short paragraphs for long reading.** Use long paragraphs; short ones are better for skimming on a phone.
- **Treating an edited transcript as a quote source.** For quotes, go back to the original wording.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does Transcriptly remove filler words automatically?

No. It removes caption artefacts (like duplicated words from rolling auto-captions) but keeps everything the speaker actually said. That's deliberate: automated filler removal can change meaning.

### Can I add my own headings inside Transcriptly?

Not directly. Edit an exported TXT file and re-upload it through **Upload / Paste Subtitles**.

### Can I share the reading version?

Only if you have the rights to. A private reading copy for your own study is very different from publishing someone else's lecture. See our [copyright page](/copyright).
