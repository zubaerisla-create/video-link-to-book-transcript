A good summary of a lecture lets you decide whether to watch it, remember it a month later, or explain it to someone else. Writing one from the video alone means constant pausing and rewinding. Writing one from a transcript is much faster — as long as you have a method. This guide gives you one, and explains where AI summarisers help and where they can quietly mislead you.

> [!note] Transcriptly doesn't generate summaries. It gives you the full, unaltered transcript in readable paragraphs, which is the raw material for the methods below — whether you summarise by hand or with another tool.

## What kind of summary do you need?

"Summary" covers several different things. Decide which one before you start, because they're written differently.

| Type | Length | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **One-line gist** | 1–2 sentences | Deciding whether a video is worth watching; labelling it in a library |
| **Abstract** | 100–200 words | Recalling the overall argument; sharing with others |
| **Section summary** | A few lines per part | Revision; navigating a long lecture |
| **Key-points list** | 5–10 bullets | Quick review before an exam or meeting |

For most educational videos, a **section summary with a one-line gist on top** is the most useful combination.

## Step 1: Get a clean transcript

Extract the transcript on the [Transcriptly home page](/) and switch to **Paragraphs** with **long** paragraph length — longer paragraphs make the structure of a talk easier to see. Keep the **Timestamped** view or an SRT export handy so each section of your summary can point back to the video.

If the captions are auto-generated, skim for mangled terms first. A summary built on a misheard keyword spreads the error. See [How to Fix Incorrect YouTube Captions](/guides/how-to-fix-incorrect-youtube-captions).

## Step 2: Find the structure

Most lectures and tutorials follow a pattern, even if the speaker doesn't announce it. Read through quickly and mark:

- **Signposts** — "first", "the second thing", "now let's move on to", "to recap". These usually mark section boundaries.
- **Definitions** — "what we mean by X is…".
- **Claims and their support** — "this matters because…", "the evidence for this is…".
- **Examples** — usually worth one line in a summary, not a paragraph.
- **Asides** — jokes, admin notices, sponsor messages. Skip them.

Write the section boundaries down with timestamps. A 50-minute lecture typically has four to eight sections.

## Step 3: Summarise each section in your own words

For each section, write two to four sentences that answer: *What is the main point, and why does it matter?* Close the transcript while you write each one, then check it. Writing from memory forces you to understand the point instead of copying sentences, and it's how you find out whether you did understand it.

A worked example, from a lecture section on spaced repetition (illustrative, not quoted from a real video):

> **Transcript (excerpt, cleaned):** "…so if you review something the day after you learn it, then three days later, then a week later, each review takes less effort and the memory lasts longer. Whereas if you do all of that reviewing in one night, you feel like you know it the next morning, but a week later most of it's gone…"
>
> **Section summary (12:40–18:05):** Spreading reviews out over increasing intervals produces longer-lasting memory than massed review, even though massed review *feels* more effective in the short term.

Note what the summary does: it states the claim, keeps the important contrast, drops the filler, and records the timestamps.

## Step 4: Write the gist last

Once the sections are done, write one or two sentences covering the whole video. It's much easier at the end than at the beginning, because you now know what the parts add up to.

## Step 5: Check against the source

Re-read the transcript quickly with your summary beside it. Look for:

- Claims the speaker made more cautiously than your summary suggests ("may", "in some studies").
- Numbers you've rounded or misremembered.
- Sections you merged that the speaker deliberately kept separate.

## Using AI summarisers responsibly

You may want to paste a transcript into an AI tool to get a first draft. That can save time, especially for long videos, but transcripts have specific properties that make AI summaries go wrong in predictable ways:

- **Errors in, errors out.** If auto-captions turned a drug name or a statistic into the wrong word, the summariser will confidently repeat the wrong word.
- **No speaker labels.** YouTube captions usually don't say who's speaking. In interviews and panels, a summariser can attribute the host's question to the guest, or one guest's opinion to another.
- **Hedging disappears.** Summaries tend to turn "this might suggest" into "this shows".
- **The middle gets squeezed.** Long inputs are often summarised unevenly, with more detail from the beginning and end.
- **Invented connections.** A tool can add context that sounds plausible but was never said.

If you use one, treat its output as a draft and do Step 5 thoroughly. Ask it to include timestamps or quotes for each point, then spot-check those against the transcript. Never cite an AI summary as what a speaker said.

## Common mistakes

- **Summarising the first ten minutes in detail and the rest in a line.** Allocate length by importance, not by order.
- **Copying sentences from the transcript.** That's extracting, not summarising — and you won't remember it.
- **Leaving out timestamps.** Without them, the summary can't take you back to the explanation when you need it.
- **Summarising a translation as if it were the original.** If you worked from a translated transcript, note that in the summary.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a summary of a one-hour lecture be?

For a section summary, roughly 200–400 words is usually enough. If it's much longer, you're probably writing notes rather than a summary — which is fine, but give it a different label.

### Should I summarise in the lecture's language or my own?

If you're learning the subject, summarise in the language you think in. If you're learning the *language*, summarising in the target language is excellent practice — see [How to Learn English Using YouTube Transcripts](/guides/how-to-learn-english-using-youtube-transcripts).

### Can I share my summary publicly?

Your own summary in your own words, credited to the original video, is generally fine. Publishing the full transcript is a different matter; see our [copyright page](/copyright).
