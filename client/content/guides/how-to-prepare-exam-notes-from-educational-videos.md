Exam notes are not the same as lecture notes. Lecture notes record what was taught; exam notes are designed to get it into your memory and out again under pressure. If your course relies on video lectures — or you're preparing for a test using YouTube explanations — transcripts let you build exam material much faster than rewatching. This guide shows how.

## The principle: test, don't reread

Learning research has consistently found that two techniques work particularly well for exams:

- **Retrieval practice** — trying to recall information from memory (answering questions, flashcards, blank-page recall) rather than rereading it.
- **Spacing** — spreading study sessions over days and weeks rather than cramming.

Rereading and highlighting feel productive but tend to produce weaker long-term results. So the goal of exam notes is to turn lectures into things you can *test yourself on*, scheduled over time.

## Step 1: Gather your sources

For each lecture or video in the exam's scope:

1. Extract the transcript on the [Transcriptly home page](/) (or with the [Chrome extension](/guides/how-to-use-chrome-extension)).
2. Save it to your library with a consistent title — `CHEM 110 L03 — Chemical bonding` — so you can find any lecture quickly.
3. Collect the matching slides, readings and any past papers.

If you already have structured notes, start from those. If not, build them first with [How to Turn YouTube Lectures into Structured Study Notes](/guides/how-to-turn-youtube-lectures-into-study-notes).

## Step 2: Find what's examinable

Lecturers often signal what matters, and transcripts make those signals searchable. Use the in-transcript search for phrases like:

- "exam", "test", "midterm", "final"
- "important", "make sure you", "you need to know"
- "common mistake", "people often get this wrong"
- "definition", "we define"

Every hit is a candidate for your exam notes. Also note topics that come up across several lectures — repetition is a strong signal.

## Step 3: Condense to one page per lecture

For each lecture, write a single page:

- **Top:** a two-sentence summary of the lecture.
- **Middle:** definitions, formulas, key claims and one example each.
- **Bottom:** anything the lecturer flagged as important, and your own questions.

One page forces you to choose. If it doesn't fit, you haven't condensed enough. Keep the lecture's timestamp next to each item so you can return to the full explanation.

## Step 4: Turn every item into a question

This is the most important step. For each item on the page, write a question that requires you to *produce* the answer, not recognise it.

| Note | Weak question | Strong question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ionic bonds form between metals and non-metals via electron transfer | "Ionic bonds involve electron transfer — true or false?" | "How does an ionic bond form, and between which kinds of elements?" |
| Le Chatelier's principle | "What is Le Chatelier's principle?" | "If you increase the pressure on this gaseous equilibrium, which way does it shift and why?" |

Mix question types:

- **Recall:** definitions and facts.
- **Explain:** why something happens.
- **Apply:** use a concept on a new example — the type most exams reward.
- **Compare:** how two concepts differ.

Transcripts are particularly good for "explain" questions, because they contain the lecturer's own explanation, which you can check your answer against.

## Step 5: Schedule your reviews

A simple schedule that works for most courses:

- **Day 1:** build the page and questions; answer them once.
- **Day 2 or 3:** answer from memory; mark the ones you got wrong.
- **About a week later:** answer again; focus on the ones you got wrong.
- **Then every week or two until the exam.**

Each time, answer before checking. For anything you got wrong, go back to the transcript at the timestamp and reread the explanation, then write a better question if the original was ambiguous.

## Step 6: Practise under exam conditions

In the final week or two:

- Do past papers without notes, timed.
- Try **blank-page recall**: for each lecture, write everything you remember on a blank page in five minutes, then check against your one-page summary.
- Explain topics aloud as if teaching. If you get stuck, that's what to review next.

## Common mistakes

- **Making beautiful notes and never testing.** Notes are a means, not the end.
- **Questions that only need recognition.** True/false and "what is X?" are easier than the exam will be.
- **Cramming.** One extra spaced session usually beats an extra late-night session.
- **Trusting a caption error.** Check key terms and numbers against your slides or textbook before memorising them.
- **Ignoring the lecturer's hints.** If they said "this will be on the exam", believe them.

## A realistic timeline

For a course with twelve one-hour video lectures and an exam in four weeks:

- **Week 1:** one-page summaries and question sets for lectures 1–6 (about an hour each, less as you get faster).
- **Week 2:** same for lectures 7–12; first review of 1–6.
- **Week 3:** reviews of everything, focusing on weak questions; first past paper.
- **Week 4:** past papers, blank-page recall, final reviews of weak areas.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I use flashcards or a question list?

Either works. Flashcards are easier to shuffle and space; a question list is faster to write. What matters is that you answer from memory.

### Can I use AI to write my questions?

It can produce a first draft, but check every question against the transcript. AI tools sometimes create questions about things the lecture didn't actually say, especially when the captions contain errors.

### What if the lectures don't match the exam syllabus?

The syllabus wins. Use transcripts to find where each syllabus item is covered, and look elsewhere for anything that isn't.
