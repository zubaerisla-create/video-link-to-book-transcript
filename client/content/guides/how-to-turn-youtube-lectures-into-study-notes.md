A transcript is not a set of notes. It's everything the lecturer said, including false starts, tangents and repetition, in the order they happened to say it. Notes are what you get when you decide what mattered and organise it so you can learn from it. This guide shows how to get from one to the other efficiently, with a worked example and three note formats to choose from.

## What good lecture notes do

Good notes:

- **Capture the structure** — the main topics and how they relate.
- **Keep definitions and key claims precise.**
- **Include one example** per idea, not all of them.
- **Point back to the source** so you can rewatch an explanation.
- **Are much shorter than the transcript** — often a tenth of the length or less.

## Step 1: Prepare the transcript

1. Paste the lecture link into the [Transcriptly home page](/) and extract the transcript.
2. Use **Paragraphs** view with **long** paragraph length to see the shape of the lecture.
3. Keep the **Timestamped** view (or an SRT export) available to look up times.
4. If the captions are auto-generated, have the slides or textbook nearby to check terms.

## Step 2: Skim for structure (10 minutes)

Read the first sentence of each paragraph and mark section boundaries. Lecturers usually signal them: "So that's X. Now let's talk about Y", "The second reason is…", "Let's look at an example". Write a skeleton outline with timestamps:

```
00:00  Intro + admin (skip)
03:15  1. What is a market equilibrium
14:40  2. Shifts in demand vs movement along the curve
27:05  3. Price ceilings and shortages
41:30  4. Worked example: rent control
49:10  Recap
```

This skeleton is the backbone of your notes.

## Step 3: Fill each section

For each section, read the transcript passage and write:

- **The main idea** in one or two sentences, in your own words.
- **Definitions**, as close to the lecturer's wording as needed for precision.
- **One example**, briefly.
- **Anything the lecturer flagged** — "this is important", "people often get this wrong", "this will come up later".

Leave out repetition, asides and admin. If the lecturer explains the same idea three times, keep the clearest version.

### Worked example

Here's a cleaned transcript excerpt (illustrative) and the notes it becomes.

> "…okay so a lot of people mix these two things up, so let's be really careful. When the price of coffee changes, people buy more or less coffee — that's a movement along the demand curve, the curve itself hasn't moved. But if, say, a study comes out saying coffee is great for you, then at every price people want more coffee, so the whole curve shifts to the right. That's a shift in demand. Right? Movement along versus shift. This is going to come up on the midterm, I promise you…"

**Notes:**

```
2. Shifts in demand vs movement along the curve  [14:40]
- Movement ALONG the curve: caused by a change in the good's own price.
- SHIFT of the curve: caused by anything else that changes demand at
  every price (tastes, income, prices of related goods, info).
  e.g. health study on coffee → demand shifts right.
- ⚠ Lecturer: "will come up on the midterm".
```

About 90 words of speech became about 50 words of notes, with the important distinction, an example, the timestamp and the exam warning.

## Step 4: Choose a note format

Different formats suit different subjects. Three that work well with transcripts:

### Outline notes

Hierarchical bullets following the lecture's structure, as in the example above. Best for lectures with clear sections — most science, economics and technical subjects.

### Cornell notes

Split a page into three areas:

- **Notes column** (right): the content, as above.
- **Cue column** (left): a question or keyword for each chunk ("What's the difference between a shift and a movement?").
- **Summary** (bottom): two or three sentences for the whole page.

The cue column turns your notes into self-test material. Cover the right side and answer the cues from memory.

### Concept maps

Boxes for concepts, arrows labelled with relationships ("causes", "is a type of", "contradicts"). Best for subjects where ideas interconnect more than they follow a sequence — history, biology systems, theory-heavy courses. Build the map *after* outline notes, as a second pass.

## Step 5: Add your own connections

What makes notes yours is the part that wasn't in the lecture:

- How does this connect to last week's topic?
- Where does it agree or disagree with the textbook?
- What still confuses you? (Write it down as a question.)

These connections are where most of the learning happens.

## Step 6: Keep notes and source linked

Save the transcript to your Transcriptly library (free account) with a title that matches your notes, such as `ECON 101 L05 — Supply and demand`. When a note doesn't make sense later, you can find the full explanation in seconds. Timestamps in your notes take you back to the video when you need a diagram.

## Common mistakes

- **Copying the transcript into your notes.** If your notes are half the length of the transcript, you haven't decided what matters yet.
- **Skipping the skeleton.** Without structure first, notes come out as a long list of disconnected facts.
- **No timestamps.** You'll waste time searching later.
- **Ignoring caption errors.** A misheard term copied into notes becomes something you "learned".
- **Only taking notes.** Notes are for review. Pair them with self-testing — see [How to Prepare Exam Notes from Educational Videos](/guides/how-to-prepare-exam-notes-from-educational-videos).

## How long should this take?

For a dense one-hour lecture, expect perhaps 30–45 minutes the first few times, getting faster as you learn to spot structure. That's still usually quicker than rewatching with pauses, and the result is far more useful.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I take notes while watching or afterwards?

With a transcript, mostly afterwards. While watching, note only timestamps and questions; build the real notes from the transcript.

### Handwritten or typed?

Either works. Some people find handwriting forces more summarising because it's slower. Typing is easier to search and reorganise. The important part is writing in your own words.

### What about lectures with lots of slides and diagrams?

Use the transcript for the explanation and the slides for the visuals. Note slide numbers alongside timestamps.
