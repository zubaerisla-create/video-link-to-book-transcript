Hindi and Arabic are two of the most widely spoken languages in the world, and both have enormous amounts of video on YouTube. They also pose very different challenges for learners using transcripts: Hindi is written in Devanagari from left to right and is closely related to Urdu; Arabic is written right to left, usually without short vowels, and the language spoken in most videos differs from the written standard. This guide covers how to use video transcripts for each, and the specific pitfalls to watch for.

## What the two languages have in common for learners

- **A new script.** Both require learning to read a non-Latin script before transcripts become really useful.
- **Spoken vs written gaps.** Both have significant differences between how people speak and how texts are written.
- **Variable caption quality.** Creator captions are excellent when they exist; auto-captions and translations are less predictable than for English.

## Part 1: Hindi

### What to expect from Hindi transcripts

- **Script.** Most Hindi captions are in Devanagari (हिन्दी). Some creators caption in romanised Hindi ("Hinglish" spelling) instead, which is easier to read at first but inconsistent and less useful long-term.
- **Sentence marks.** Hindi uses the **पूर्ण विराम (।)** as a full stop. Transcriptly's paragraph builder treats it as a sentence boundary, as it does for Bangla.
- **Code-mixing.** Urban Hindi speech mixes in a lot of English. Captions may write those words in Devanagari (कंप्यूटर) or in Latin letters.
- **Hindi and Urdu.** Spoken Hindi and Urdu are closely related and largely mutually intelligible in everyday conversation, but use different scripts and draw formal vocabulary from different sources. A Hindi video's captions may be in Devanagari while a similar Urdu video is in Perso-Arabic script. Make sure you're studying the one you intend to.

### Choosing Hindi videos

- **News and educational channels** usually speak clearly and often provide captions.
- **Explainer channels** on science, history or exams are excellent for formal vocabulary.
- **Film and drama clips** are useful later, for natural conversation, but are fast and often uncaptioned.

### A Hindi study session

1. Extract the transcript on the [Transcriptly home page](/) in original-language mode and confirm the language label is Hindi.
2. Listen once without text.
3. Read the Devanagari transcript. If you're still slow at reading, read it aloud, syllable by syllable.
4. Use **Side-by-Side** translation into English to check difficult passages.
5. Record useful words with the sentence from the video, and note the gender of nouns — Hindi verbs and adjectives agree with gender, so knowing a noun's gender is part of knowing the word.
6. Shadow a short section to practise rhythm and the retroflex consonants (ट, ड, ण).

## Part 2: Arabic

### What to expect from Arabic transcripts

- **Direction.** Arabic is written right to left. Transcriptly displays Arabic-script text using the browser's normal bidirectional text handling; mixed Arabic-and-English lines can look jumbled in any app, so read them slowly.
- **Missing short vowels.** Everyday Arabic writing omits most short-vowel marks (*harakat*). Captions usually do too, so you need to know a word to pronounce it confidently. Educational and Qur'anic content is more likely to include vowel marks.
- **Punctuation.** Arabic uses its own question mark (**؟**) and comma (،). Transcriptly's sentence splitting recognises the Arabic question mark as a sentence ending; Urdu's full stop (۔) is recognised too.
- **Diglossia.** This is the biggest issue. **Modern Standard Arabic (MSA)** is used in news, formal speeches and most writing. Most conversation — vlogs, dramas, interviews — is in a **regional dialect** (Egyptian, Levantine, Gulf, Maghrebi, and others), which can differ substantially from MSA and from each other.

### Choosing Arabic videos

Decide first which variety you're learning:

| Goal | Best video types |
| --- | --- |
| Modern Standard Arabic | News bulletins, documentaries, formal lectures, educational channels |
| A specific dialect | Vlogs, dramas, comedy and interviews from that region |
| Religious or classical texts | Recitations and lessons with vowelled captions |

Captions for dialect videos are often written inconsistently, since dialects lack a single standard spelling. MSA content tends to have cleaner captions.

### An Arabic study session

1. Extract the transcript in original-language mode; check the label says Arabic.
2. Listen once without text.
3. Read the transcript. For unvowelled words you don't know, look up the **root** (usually three consonants) — Arabic dictionaries are often organised by root, and recognising roots is one of the most powerful skills in Arabic.
4. Use **Side-by-Side** translation to check meaning, keeping in mind that machine translation of dialect is less reliable than of MSA.
5. Record words with their root, the vowelled form (from a dictionary), and the sentence from the video.

Example entry:

| Field | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Word | مدرسة |
| Vowelled | مَدْرَسَة (madrasa) |
| Root | د-ر-س (d-r-s, "study") |
| Related | دَرْس (lesson), مُدَرِّس (teacher) |
| Sentence from the video | (copy from the transcript) |

## Using translation responsibly

For both languages, Transcriptly's translation mode uses YouTube's own translated track when one is available, and otherwise a general-purpose machine translation service. That's helpful for getting the gist, but:

- Idioms, honorifics and dialect words are often mistranslated.
- Alignment is paragraph by paragraph, not word by word.
- Never treat the translation as a reliable gloss of a specific word. Use a dictionary for that.

## Keeping a vocabulary list

Transcriptly's tap-to-look-up dictionary is designed for **English** words (with Bangla meanings), so for Hindi and Arabic, keep your own list in a notebook, spreadsheet or flashcard app. The method in [How to Build a Personal Vocabulary Dictionary from Video Transcripts](/guides/how-to-build-a-vocabulary-dictionary-from-video-transcripts) — save selectively, keep the sentence, review with spacing — applies just the same.

## When captions aren't available

Many Hindi and Arabic videos have no captions, or only auto-translated ones. Your options are covered in [How to Transcribe Videos in Different Languages](/guides/how-to-transcribe-videos-in-different-languages). In short: look for a video with creator captions on the same topic, or use speech recognition where it's available — but expect more errors, especially for dialects.

## Common mistakes

- **Studying from auto-translated captions.** Always confirm the track is in the original language.
- **Mixing Arabic varieties unintentionally.** Know whether a video is MSA or dialect.
- **Ignoring gender (Hindi) or roots (Arabic).** They're central to how each language works.
- **Relying on romanisation.** It helps briefly, but reading the real script is what unlocks most material.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I export Hindi and Arabic transcripts?

Yes. TXT, SRT and JSON exports are UTF-8 encoded and preserve Devanagari and Arabic script.

### Does Transcriptly support Urdu?

Yes, Urdu caption tracks are handled like any other language, and Urdu is one of the languages available as a translation target.

### Which is easier to learn from video, Hindi or Arabic?

It depends on your background, but for most learners Hindi transcripts are quicker to use because the script represents vowels fully and spoken and written Hindi are closer than spoken dialect and written MSA.
