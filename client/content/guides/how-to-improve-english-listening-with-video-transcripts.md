Many English learners can read far better than they can listen. They know the words, but in real speech the words run together, vowels shrink, and sounds disappear. Transcripts are the bridge: they show you exactly what was said, so you can work out why you didn't hear it. Used carelessly, though, they become a crutch — you read instead of listening. This guide gives you exercises that use transcripts to *train* your ear, not replace it.

## Why natural English is hard to hear

Most listening problems come from a few features of connected speech:

- **Linking** — words join together: "turn it off" sounds like "tur-ni-toff".
- **Reduction** — unstressed words shrink: "going to" → "gonna", "want to" → "wanna", "and" → "n".
- **Weak forms** — small grammar words lose their vowels: "can" in "I can go" often sounds like "kn".
- **Elision** — sounds disappear: "next day" → "nex day", "last night" → "las night".
- **Stress and rhythm** — English stresses content words and rushes everything else, so the "small" words are the hardest to catch.

You may know every word in a sentence and still not recognise it, because you've only learned the *careful* pronunciation. The exercises below teach you the *real* one.

## Setting up

1. Choose a short video (3–10 minutes) at a level where you understand the general topic. See the level guide in [How to Learn English Using YouTube Transcripts](/guides/how-to-learn-english-using-youtube-transcripts).
2. Extract the transcript on the [Transcriptly home page](/) in original-language mode.
3. Use the **Timestamped** view — each segment shows its start time, so you can jump to exact moments in the video.
4. Prefer videos with **creator-uploaded captions** for these exercises. Auto-captions are usually close, but when you're checking exactly what you heard, an error in the caption is confusing.

## Exercise 1: Listen first, check second

The basic pattern for every session:

1. Listen to a 30–60 second section **without** text.
2. Write down, or say, what you understood.
3. Read that section of the transcript.
4. Listen again while reading.
5. Listen a final time without text.

Step 3 is where learning happens: you see the gap between what you heard and what was said. Step 5 checks whether you can now hear it.

## Exercise 2: Gap listening

Copy a paragraph of the transcript and delete every fifth or sixth word — especially small words like *to*, *the*, *have*, *of*, *would*. Then listen and fill in the gaps.

```
I think the main reason ___ people struggle with this ___ that
they've never ___ taught how ___ actually works.
```

Answer: "that", "is", "been", "it". These are exactly the words that reduction hides. Gap listening trains you to hear them.

## Exercise 3: Dictation

Dictation is slow but extremely effective for intermediate learners.

1. Play one sentence (5–10 seconds). Pause.
2. Write exactly what you heard. Replay up to three times.
3. Compare with the transcript and mark every difference.

Then categorise your errors. After a few sessions, a pattern appears — perhaps you always miss past-tense endings, or "can" vs "can't", or words before a vowel. That pattern tells you what to practise.

## Exercise 4: Shadowing

Shadowing means speaking along with the speaker, a fraction of a second behind, copying their rhythm and intonation.

1. Read a short section of the transcript aloud, slowly, to understand it.
2. Play it and speak along with the text in front of you.
3. Repeat until you can keep up.
4. Try it without the text.

Shadowing helps listening because it makes you *produce* the reductions and linking yourself. Once you've said "wanna" naturally, you'll hear it naturally.

## Exercise 5: Noticing reductions

Take a transcript paragraph and, while listening, mark every place where the speech differs from the written form:

| Written | Heard |
| --- | --- |
| going to | gonna |
| a lot of | a lotta |
| did you | didja |
| I don't know | I dunno |
| What do you want | Whaddaya want |

You'll start recognising these patterns in new videos almost immediately.

## Exercise 6: Extensive listening

Intensive exercises build accuracy; extensive listening builds fluency. Two or three times a week, watch something longer that you enjoy, at a comfortable level, **without** subtitles. Afterwards, skim the transcript to check the main points. If you understood the gist, the level is right.

## Weaning yourself off subtitles

Transcripts are a training tool. A sensible progression:

1. **Read before watching** — for difficult material, so the vocabulary is familiar.
2. **Watch without text, check after** — the default for most sessions.
3. **Watch without text, check only confusing parts** — as you improve.
4. **Watch without text, no check** — for comfortable material.

If you always watch with English subtitles on, you're mostly practising reading. Use the transcript *after* listening, not during, except in shadowing.

## A weekly plan

| Day | Activity | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mon | Exercise 1 + gap listening on a new video | 20 min |
| Tue | Dictation, 10 sentences from the same video | 20 min |
| Wed | Shadowing, 2 minutes of the same video | 15 min |
| Thu | Extensive listening, something you enjoy | 30 min |
| Fri | Exercise 1 on a second video | 20 min |
| Sat | Shadowing + noticing reductions | 15 min |
| Sun | Rewatch Monday's video without text | 10 min |

Working on the same video for several days is deliberate: repetition is how the patterns become automatic.

## Using auto-captions carefully

Auto-captions are usually a good guide, but they can be wrong exactly where listening is hardest — fast speech, names, overlapping speakers. If the transcript says something that doesn't make sense, trust your ears and listen again. [YouTube Auto Captions vs Whisper](/guides/youtube-auto-captions-vs-whisper) explains why these errors happen.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading while listening every time.** You'll improve your reading, not your listening.
- **Choosing material that's too hard.** If you understand almost nothing without the text, choose something easier.
- **Too much variety.** Listening to the same short clip several times teaches more than one listen to many clips.
- **Ignoring the small words.** They carry the grammar, and they're what you most need to train.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I slow the video down?

At first, slightly slower playback (0.75×) can help with dictation. Return to normal speed as soon as possible, since slowed audio changes the rhythm you're trying to learn.

### How long until I notice improvement?

Most learners notice a difference within a few weeks of regular practice, especially with dictation and shadowing. Progress then continues more gradually.

### Is it better to use British or American videos?

Use both if you can. The connected-speech patterns are similar, and exposure to several accents makes you a more flexible listener.
